# Detail and vascularity: separation, striations, definition, vascularity

This file explains the "detail" terms of bodybuilding and visible veins. For each term it separates three things: (a) the visual impression, (b) the cause that the community claims, and (c) what academic evidence supports. Physiology claims cite academic sources only. Usage claims cite federation rules, judges, coaches, and bodybuilding publications.

- Neighbor terms are in other files: `conditioning.md` (dry, crispy, conditioning), `fullness-water.md` (full, flat, spilled over, pump), and `maturity-shape.md` (mature muscle, density, grainy).
- **[INFERENCE]** marks our own reasoning. No source states it.
- "No direct evidence found" means that we searched and found no study.
- Body-fat percentages in coaching sources are community estimates. They are not measured thresholds.
- Appendix A (muscles) and Appendix B (veins) give anatomy for the 3D model.

## Key findings

1. **Separation and striations are different concepts.** Rules and judges treat them as two levels. NPC Figure asks for "separation but no visible striations" [1]. An NPC judge wants "deep, clean separation between the individual muscles, and smaller lines, or striations, across the muscle belly itself" [2]. Separation follows the borders of whole muscles or heads. Striations are fine lines inside one muscle. Both depend on a thin fat layer. Surgical studies show this for border grooves [3][4]. For striations, only coaches report it: they need extreme leanness [5] and a flexed muscle [6]. No study has found what the lines are. Fascicle bundles and their connective-tissue septa are the most plausible structure [INFERENCE from sources 7, 8, 9].
2. **Vein visibility has several supported factors.** Obesity reduces the number of veins seen by eye [10][11]. How a vein looks depends on its depth and diameter and on skin optics [12]. So a thinner fat layer over a vein should show it better [INFERENCE]. Darker skin shows fewer veins to the eye [10]. Venous filling changes fast: heat enlarges superficial veins [13][14][15], muscle contraction fills forearm veins [16], and a hanging arm or a tourniquet distends them [13]. Cold and stress make them smaller [15][13]. Vein layout differs a lot between people [16][17]. Endurance training expands blood volume [18], but no study links this to visible veins. No direct evidence was found for claims about skin thickness or collagen.
3. **"Vascularity means dry" is not supported.** The main peak-week review says that no research links visual "dry" checks to measured body fluid [19]. Clinical data point the other way for dehydration: dehydration and low blood volume make veins harder to find [11][20]. Oedema (tissue swelling) is also a risk factor for hard-to-find veins [20]. No study shows that the small fluid changes of peak week change vein visibility. Visible veins mainly reflect thin fat plus venous filling, and both can change within minutes [INFERENCE from the sources above].
4. **The "Christmas tree" is a pattern of muscle borders.** It is not a separate muscle. It is not a fat pattern. A coach describes it as the erector spinae meeting the origin of the lats [21]. Dorian Yates credits genetics and erector development [22]. Anatomy agrees with this picture: the lats start from the thoracolumbar fascia, their lowest fibers run almost straight up, and the erector spinae form rounded ridges in the lower back [23][24][25]. The fascia between them is roughly diamond-shaped [26]. The visible "tree" is these borders seen through very thin fat [INFERENCE]. No academic study of the term was found.

## Terms

### 1. Separation ("cuts")

1. **Meaning** – Visible borders between whole muscles, or between the heads of one muscle. A "separated" physique shows each muscle as its own shape. "Cuts" usually names the same lines.
2. **Visible features** – Grooves and shadow lines where two muscles meet. Typical places: ab "cuts" along the linea alba, linea semilunaris, and tendinous intersections [3]; lines between delts, biceps, and triceps [27]; quad–hamstring and glute–hamstring lines [27][28]; the pec–delt tie-in [29]. The grooves follow the outline of a whole muscle. They are wider and deeper than striations.
3. **Confused neighbors** – *Striations* are lines inside one muscle. *Definition* is the umbrella term. *Tie-in* is the place where two muscles meet; one division wants a "visible tie-in" and another wants none [27][1]. *Conditioning* is overall leanness; WFF defines "condition" as separation itself [30] (see `conditioning.md`).
4. **Consistency of usage** – Mostly consistent. IFBB lists separation with density and definition as "muscle quality" [29]. USBF defines conditioning as "differentiation between adjacent muscles" [27]. One coach uses "separation" loosely for all surface "texture", striations included [31].
5. **Causes: supported vs. speculative**
   - Supported: Contest preparation removes subcutaneous fat. In elite bodybuilders the sum of 8 skinfolds fell from 51.1 to 36.7 mm [32]. A natural competitor fell from 14.8% to 4.5% body fat [33]. Surgeons make abdominal "etching" by removing fat over the natural grooves [3] and "define the 3-dimensional surface musculature" the same way [4]. So the fat layer controls how deep the grooves look.
   - Plausible, unproven: A bodybuilding writer says that, for definition, competitors try to drain water "from between their muscles and skin" [6]. No study measured this.
   - Genetics: coaches name muscle-belly shape and insertions [31]. No academic study of separation was found.
6. **Honest illustrative representation** – Keep bones and muscle positions fixed. Deepen the existing valleys at muscle borders. Let each valley take its shape from the two rounded bellies that meet there, so it reads as a valley, not as a narrow crack. Thin a "subcutaneous layer" more over borders than over bellies, because fat fills these grooves [INFERENCE from sources 3, 4]. Best regions: abs, delt–arm borders, quad heads above the knee, glute–hamstring fold, serratus and oblique slips (Appendix A).
7. **Classification** – Distinct concept; visual description.

### 2. Striations

1. **Meaning** – Fine, roughly parallel lines across the surface of one muscle belly. They follow the fiber direction of the muscle. They show only on very lean muscle, mostly when it is flexed.
2. **Visible features** – Many thin grooves inside a muscle outline: a fan of lines toward the armpit on the pecs; angled lines on the front and side delts; oblique lines on the quads (see Feathering); lines on the triceps and glutes. A pro names pecs, delts, triceps, and quads [34]. A federation asks for "glute detail" [28]. No source was found that ranks hamstrings or traps. The lines "ripple when flexed" [6].
3. **Confused neighbors** – *Separation* (borders between muscles). *Grainy* (overall granular texture; see `maturity-shape.md`). *Feathering* (quad striations). *Histological striations*: microscopic bands of sarcomeres about 2 µm long that give "striated muscle" its name [7]. They cannot be seen through skin [INFERENCE].
4. **Consistency of usage** – Consistent as a judging term, one level beyond separation. NPC Figure: separation "but no visible striations" [1]. NPC Women's Physique: a "small amount of striations is acceptable", but not "overly striated" [35]. IFBB Women's Physique: "free from deep muscle separation and/or striations" [36]. WFF: "intra-muscular striations" [30].
5. **Causes: supported vs. speculative**
   - Supported (indirect): Contest preparation reaches very low fat [32][33]. Muscles are bundles of fascicles wrapped in perimysium [7]. Gluteus maximus is "remarkably coarse", with "large bundles separated by fibrous septa" [8]. Bodybuilders have steeper fiber angles [9]. Contraction shortens fascicles and steepens their angle [37].
   - Plausible, unproven: The lines are fascicle bundles or septa seen through thin skin [INFERENCE]. A coach says that not all very lean pros show striations, and he credits better muscle "architecture" [31].
   - Contested folklore: Special training "creates" striations. Lee Haney says varied exercises give more [34]. A strength coach calls high-rep striation training "a myth" [5]. No academic evidence either way.
6. **Honest illustrative representation** – Use a fine normal-map line pattern that follows each muscle's fiber direction (Appendix A). Mask it to the muscle belly and fade it near tendons. Link it to two sliders: subcutaneous layer (thin = visible) and flex (on = sharper lines, slightly steeper angle [37]). Best regions: pecs, front and side delts, triceps, quads, glutes. Do not draw microscopic cross-bands.
7. **Classification** – Distinct concept; visual description; contested explanation.

### 3. Cross-striations

1. **Meaning** – In bodybuilding writing, a rare variant of "striations". In anatomy, "cross-striated" describes the microscopic band pattern of a skeletal muscle fiber [38].
2. **Visible features** – When a bodybuilder uses the term, it names the same lines as striations, on the pecs, delts, triceps, and quads [34]. No source describes a separate pattern, such as lines that cross other lines.
3. **Confused neighbors** – Striations; grainy; the histology term (sarcomere bands) [7].
4. **Consistency of usage** – Rare, overlapping slang. Lee Haney uses it as a synonym for striations [34]. The rulebooks that we checked say "striations", not "cross-striations" [1][36][30].
5. **Causes: supported vs. speculative** – The same as striations. The histology meaning has a known cause: the repeating, aligned sarcomeres of each fiber [7][38]. That banding is far below the scale of anything visible on skin [INFERENCE]. Do not link the two meanings.
6. **Honest illustrative representation** – Do not build a separate effect. Map the term to the striation effect. In the app text, say that the histology term means a different thing.
7. **Classification** – Overlapping slang.

### 4. Definition

1. **Meaning** – How clearly the outlines and inner details of muscles show. It is an umbrella term. A "defined" physique shows muscle shapes instead of a smooth surface.
2. **Visible features** – Clear muscle edges, visible abs, separation lines, and at the extreme, striations and veins [39][6]. A glossary defines it as "the absence of fat over clearly delineated muscular movement" [39].
3. **Confused neighbors** – *Conditioning* (WFF uses "condition" for definition or separation [30]; a review calls conditioning "low body fat levels" [19]). *Separation*, *detail*, *striations*. *Ripped, shredded, peeled, diced* are degree words for very high definition [1]. *Muscularity*: a glossary treats definition as "muscularity" [39], but IFBB judges muscular bulk separately [29].
4. **Consistency of usage** – Mostly consistent, but broad. IFBB judges look for "muscular bulk, balanced development, muscular density and definition" [29]. The rulebook gives no formal definition of the word.
5. **Causes: supported vs. speculative**
   - Supported: Fat loss during contest preparation [32][33]. Removal of fat over muscle borders creates surgical "definition" [3][4].
   - Plausible, unproven: A tan and stage light increase contrast. A federation says that a tan helps "to highlight muscle definition" [28]. No study was found.
   - Folklore: Special "toning" or high-rep work produces definition. No direct evidence was found; a strength coach calls the striation version of this claim a myth [5].
6. **Honest illustrative representation** – Use one master slider for the subcutaneous layer of the whole body. As it thins, reduce surface smoothing, deepen the border valleys (separation), and then fade in striations and vein relief. Keep muscle volume constant. Muscle size belongs to a separate slider (`fullness-water.md`, `maturity-shape.md`).
7. **Classification** – Visual description; overlapping slang (umbrella term).

### 5. Detail

1. **Meaning** – Small visible features inside a muscle group: minor borders, striations, and tendon lines. People say "detail in the mid-back" or "abdominal detail".
2. **Visible features** – Many small lines in one region. In the back: edges of teres major, infraspinatus, and the lower-back ridges. In the abs: the intersections and the serratus and oblique slips (Appendix A). Phil Heath says an exercise "adds good detail to the mid-back" and that he needs "good etching in the lower back" [40].
3. **Confused neighbors** – Definition (broader), separation, striations, grainy, mature (`maturity-shape.md`), "etching".
4. **Consistency of usage** – Overlapping slang, used as a degree word. NPC Fit Model: the core should be "not too detailed or separated" [1]. ANBF wants "abdominal detail" and "glute detail" [28]. Lee Haney contrasts "mass" with "shape and detail" [34]. Surgeons also write about the "detail" of abdominal muscles [3].
5. **Causes: supported vs. speculative** – The same as separation and striations. No separate mechanism was found.
6. **Honest illustrative representation** – Do not add new anatomy. Raise the number of visible minor grooves in one region (teres major and infraspinatus edges, serratus slips, ab intersections). Drive it with the same subcutaneous slider. Best regions: upper and mid back, abs, glutes.
7. **Classification** – Overlapping slang; visual description.

### 6. Christmas tree (lower back)

1. **Meaning** – A pattern on the lower back of a very lean, muscular person in rear poses. It looks like a fir tree: a central trunk with angled branches.
2. **Visible features** – Two rounded vertical ridges beside the lumbar spine form the trunk; these are the erector spinae [24][25]. Angled lines run up and out from the lower back toward the sides; these follow the lat fibers that rise at an angle from the lumbar fascia, and the lat borders [23]. A flat, roughly diamond-shaped area lies over the lumbar spine and sacrum; this is the thoracolumbar fascia [26][7]. Rear lat spread and rear double biceps show it [21][40].
3. **Confused neighbors** – "Lower lats", lower-back "detail" or "etching" [40], back thickness. It is not the V-taper, which is overall back width.
4. **Consistency of usage** – Mostly consistent about the region, not about the structures. One coach names the erector spinae and the lat origin [21]. Yates names genetics and erector development [22]. Another coach names only "a defined, thick lower back" [41]. The phrase spread after Samir Bannout's lower back at the 1983 Mr. Olympia [42].
5. **Causes: supported vs. speculative**
   - Supported (anatomy): The lats start from the thoracolumbar fascia; their lowest fibers run almost straight up and the middle fibers run up at an angle [23]. The erector spinae form thick columns in the lower back under this fascia [24][25]. Thin fat is needed to see any border [32] (indirect).
   - Plausible, unproven: Individual tendon and aponeurosis layout ("genetics") decides how tree-like it looks [22].
   - Folklore: Specific exercises "etch" the tree [41]. No study was found.
   - Answer to the key question: a pattern of muscle and aponeurosis borders made visible by low fat [INFERENCE].
6. **Honest illustrative representation** – Keep anatomy fixed. Deepen the outer edges of both erector columns. Add a few crease lines that follow the lower lat fibers up and out from the iliac crest and lumbar fascia. Keep the central diamond flat and slightly glossy. Drive it with the subcutaneous slider and a rear-pose flex.
7. **Classification** – Visual description; contested explanation (which structures form it).

### 7. Feathering (quads)

1. **Meaning** – Fine, angled striations on the quadriceps that look like the barbs of a feather. It marks an extreme level of quad conditioning.
2. **Visible features** – Many small oblique lines on the front and outer thigh, and above the inner knee. A coach describes "plenty of small lines and striations along their legs" [31]. The lines meet a central or edge line at an angle; the rectus femoris has fibers on both sides of a central tendon [43][8]. They show best with the quad flexed and the knee straight [INFERENCE from sources 37].
3. **Confused neighbors** – Quad striations (same thing); quad separation (lines between quad heads); "sweep" (the outer curve of the thigh).
4. **Consistency of usage** – Overlapping slang with "quad striations". The meaning is consistent. A pro says "those feathers on the quads" [44]. USBF says Women's Physique should not look "overly feathered … like that of Bodybuilding" [27].
5. **Causes: supported vs. speculative**
   - Supported (anatomy): "Pennate" comes from *penna*, "feathers"; the rectus femoris is bipennate [43]. Gray calls its surface fibers "bipenniform" [8]. Vastus fibers run down at an angle onto aponeuroses [8]. Contraction steepens the fiber angle [37]. Hypertrophy steepens it too, at least in the triceps [9].
   - Plausible, unproven: The slang describes this real feather-like fiber layout seen through thin skin [INFERENCE]. No source states the link.
   - Coach claim: it needs very low body fat and relates to "muscle maturity" [31]. No study was found.
6. **Honest illustrative representation** – Use chevron (herringbone) normal-map lines on the rectus femoris, on both sides of a central line. Use oblique lines on the vastus lateralis and the vastus medialis that run down toward the kneecap at a shallow angle to their aponeurosis [37][8]. Steepen the angle slightly when flexed. Link to the subcutaneous and flex sliders.
7. **Classification** – Overlapping slang; visual description.

### 8. Vascularity ("vascular", "roadmap")

1. **Meaning** – Visible superficial veins under the skin. A "vascular" physique shows many prominent veins. "Roadmap" means a dense, branching network, often on the arms [45][46].
2. **Visible features** – Raised, slightly bluish, branching tubes. In healthy adults, ultrasound measures arm veins at about 3–5.5 mm [14]. Common sites: back of the hand, forearm, both sides of the biceps, the groove between delt and pec, the lower abdomen, the inner leg, and the calf (Appendix B). The blue tint is an optical effect of skin and depth [12].
3. **Confused neighbors** – *Dry* (a claim about low water under the skin; `conditioning.md`). *Pump* (short-term exercise effect; `fullness-water.md`). *Conditioning*.
4. **Consistency of usage** – Consistent meaning, contested value. One natural federation scores "Muscle Definition & Vascularity" at about 45% [47], and WFF Sports Model expects "some vascularity" [30]. IFBB and NPC women's physique rules mark it down [36][1]. IFBB men's bodybuilding rules do not name it [29].
5. **Causes: supported vs. speculative**
   - Supported: Obesity reduces the number of veins seen by eye; darker skin does too [10]. How a vein looks depends on its depth and diameter and on skin optics [12]. Heat enlarges superficial veins and raises visibility scores [13][14]. Prolonged exercise with a rising core temperature enlarges the basilic vein; cold makes arm veins smaller [15]. Fist clenching fills forearm veins [16]. A hanging arm or a tourniquet increases prominence; fear causes vasoconstriction [13]. Vein patterns differ between people and between sexes [17].
   - Plausible, unproven: Muscle growth pushes veins outward (physician opinion) [48]. Training enlarges veins: in a pilot study, veins grew in both the exercised and the non-exercised arm [49]. Endurance training expands blood volume [18]; no study links this to visibility. Squats lower plasma volume by 22% as fluid moves into the working muscle [50], so the post-workout look does not need extra blood volume [INFERENCE].
   - Folklore: "Vascular means dry" (see Key finding 3) [19][11][20]. A physician even claims that sodium retention swells veins [48]; this is also unverified.
   - Safety: Some competitors misuse diuretics to look "dry". This can cause dangerous potassium loss [51][19]. This file gives no protocols.
6. **Honest illustrative representation** – Model veins as tubes on the paths in Appendix B, under a skin layer. Use three separate sliders: subcutaneous layer (depth), venous filling (heat or pump: diameter and bulge), and skin tone with lighting contrast. Do not tie veins to a "water" slider. Best regions: hand and forearm, biceps, deltopectoral groove, lower abdomen, inner leg, calf.
7. **Classification** – Distinct concept; contested explanation (what it signals).

### Notes for striation and separation modeling

- Pennate (lines at an angle): lateral deltoid multipennate; front and back deltoid unipennate [56][43][55]. Rectus femoris surface fibers bipennate (feather pattern) [8][43]. Extensor digitorum unipennate [43].
- Parallel or fusiform (lines along the axis): biceps brachii, palmaris longus, semitendinosus, biceps femoris long head [63][64][8][43]. Gluteus maximus: coarse parallel bundles with septa [8].
- Convergent (lines radiate to one tendon): pectoralis major, trapezius, latissimus dorsi, infraspinatus, gluteus medius [52][56][23][8][43].
- Gastrocnemius fibers meet at an angle on a midline seam. Gray gives no pennation type [68].
- Rectus abdominis: intersections make zigzag cross-grooves; linea alba is a midline dip; linea semilunaris is the outer edge [58][57][61].
- Deltopectoral groove (infraclavicular fossa) between deltoid and pectoralis major; sternal furrow between pectorals [54][53][55].
- Serratus and external oblique slips make an interlocking zigzag; erector ridges flank the spinal furrow [25][57][53].
- The iliotibial groove crosses vastus lateralis; the ulnar furrow divides forearm flexors and extensors [53][65].

## Anatomy appendix B: Superficial veins often visible on lean athletes

Superficial veins lie in the fat under the skin, above the deep fascia. Their pattern varies, so treat these routes as typical paths [16][69]. Limb veins become visible when the proximal trunks are compressed [53][65]. Muscle contraction fills the forearm veins, and the veins and valves then become prominent [16]. No checked source describes vein visibility in lean athletes, so most visibility notes are [modeling inference].

### Variation between people

Upper-limb superficial veins are highly variable [16], and lower-limb veins vary more than the arteries [69]. A meta-analysis of 27 studies (9,924 arms) found the "N" elbow pattern in about 44–60% and the "M" pattern in about 20–25% [17]. "M" types were more common in males, "I" or "O" types in females, and frequencies differ between populations [17]. Sources disagree on great saphenous duplication: about 1% in the thigh [83], or 8% thigh and 25% calf [69].

## Open questions / uncertainty

- No study defines or measures separation, striations, feathering, or the "Christmas tree" on living skin. No validated scale exists. All usage evidence comes from federation rules, judges, and coaches.
- The structure behind visible striations is unknown. Candidates are fascicle bundles, perimysial septa, or both [INFERENCE]. An ultrasound or MRI study of contest-lean athletes, at sites with and without striations, could answer this.
- Body-fat numbers for striations or veins (for example "2–5%" or "around five percent") come from coaches and journalists [5][6]. Measurement methods differ, and fat at one site can differ from whole-body fat.
- Nobody has measured how "subcutaneous water" changes during peak week, or linked it to separation or vein visibility [19].
- Genetic claims (skin thickness, collagen, fascicle size, tendon and aponeurosis layout) are coach opinions [31][22]. No study compares equally lean athletes.
- Stage factors (heat from lights, backstage pump-up, tan, light angle) probably change how grooves and veins look [INFERENCE]. No study measured them in bodybuilders.
- It is not known whether the lower trapezius or other deep back muscles add to the Christmas-tree pattern.
- No source links the lower-abdomen "V" look to named veins. Appendix B marks it as a modeling inference [78].
- Anatomy sources disagree on some details: the number of rectus abdominis intersections [58][61]; external oblique interdigitations [58][62]; how often the great saphenous vein is doubled [83][69]; the level of the saphenofemoral junction [75][83][69]; where the superficial epigastric vein ends [77][78]; and where the anterior accessory saphenous vein runs [86].
- Some reference photos in the next section are small or have weak provenance (see the caveats at its end).
