# Maturity, density, shape, insertions, proportions, and size

Research notes for the Bodybuilding Appearance Explorer.

**Scope:** muscle maturity; density; hard vs. soft; grainy; size vs. shape; insertions; symmetry, balance, and proportion; aesthetics and flow; V-taper and X-frame; size, mass, and thickness.

**Method:** Every URL in *Sources* was fetched on 2026-10-09 and checked against the claim it supports. Quotes are copied from the fetched pages. Physiology claims use academic sources only. Rulebooks, judges, coaches, magazines, and one forum show usage only.

**Evidence labels:** *Supported* = academic data support it. *Plausible but unproven* = it fits known facts, but no study tested it. *Folklore* = a community claim with no support, or with contrary data. *No direct evidence found* = we searched and found no study. `[INFERENCE]` marks our own reasoning.

**Related files:** "full", "flat", "spilled", "dry", and "crispy" belong to the fullness research file. Separation, striations, and veins are in `research/detail-vascularity.md`. This file only cross-references them.

## Quick answers

### What kind of term is each?

| Term | Classification | What the evidence supports |
|---|---|---|
| Maturity | Overlapping slang; contested explanation | More muscle and less fat. No tissue change shown. |
| Density | Overlapping slang (four meanings) | Not literal density. The look tracks muscle size, low fat, and deep borders. |
| Hard vs. soft | Visual description; overlapping slang | A thin fat layer. Other causes are unproven. |
| Grainy | Visual description; contested explanation | A very thin fat layer. The anatomy of the lines is not mapped. |
| Size vs. shape | Distinct concepts | Size is trainable and heritable. Muscle shape is similar between people. Regional growth is small. |
| Insertions | Distinct concept (structural) | Belly and tendon anatomy varies. Training is not shown to move attachments. |
| Symmetry, balance, proportion | Visual description (subjective judgement); overlapping slang | Left–right asymmetry from sport is real. Ideal ratios are judgement. |
| Aesthetics, flow | Visual description (subjective judgement) | V-shape preference in perception studies. "Flow" is untested. |
| V-taper, X-frame | Visual description of the silhouette | Bone breadths are heritable. V-shape preference in perception studies. |
| Size, mass, thickness | Distinct concepts (geometry); "size" and "mass" overlap | Research measures thickness, area, and volume separately. |

### How to show "mature/grainy" vs. "smooth" at equal size and conditioning

1. Lock the skeleton, the muscle volumes, the outline, and the fat layer.
2. Change only surface micro-detail. Add a fine normal-map layer whose lines follow each muscle's fiber direction.
3. Keep this layer much finer than the separation grooves between muscles.
4. Add faint secondary splits inside muscle groups: deltoid heads, triceps heads, quad heads, upper and lower pec.
5. For "smooth", blur the same layer and soften the secondary splits. Do not change size.
6. Label it as a look. Science has not found a tissue change behind "maturity" beyond more muscle and less fat [41–47, 50].
7. Do not drive this look with an "age" or "years" slider. No study links age or training years to it [69, 70].

Best regions: deltoids, upper chest and pec–delt tie-in, triceps lateral head, quads, glutes and hamstrings, mid and lower back.

### Which judging sources use "maturity" and "density"?

- "Density" is official but undefined. IFBB judges assess "muscle size and quality (density, separation, definition)" and look for "muscular bulk, balanced development, muscular density and definition" [1].
- "Maturity" is in no rulebook we found. The IFBB 2026 bodybuilding rules contain no "maturity", "grainy", "thick", "mass", "X-frame", or "flow" [1]. The IFBB Pro League rules page gives no written criteria [5].
- Judges, coaches, and pros use "maturity" in interviews: head judge Steve Weinberger [22], Jay Cutler [21], coach Milos Sarcev [24], and Shawn Ray [23].
- Natural federations define more terms. OCB puts "densely muscled" under Size and "grainy look" under Definition [8]. WNBF defines symmetry with V-taper and X-frame [7].

### Age and training years in folklore

- Folklore says maturity takes many years: "after many years of hard and heavy training" [20]; "anywhere from five to 15 years" [19].
- Writers disagree on age vs. years: "as much about your age" [18] vs. "not related to your age" [19]. Cutler ties it to being "in your early 30s" [21].
- The evidence is thin. Years of training did not differ significantly between placed and non-placed natural competitors (14.2 vs. 10.9 years, p = 0.40) [69]. Pros reported more years than amateurs, but not significantly (17.0 vs. 11.2, p = 0.083) [70]. No study links age or years to appearance.

### Suggested model controls (used in the sections below)

- **Frame:** bone breadths and lengths. Fixed per body; presets only.
- **Size:** volume per muscle.
- **Belly–tendon split:** where the fleshy belly ends on a fixed tendon path.
- **Fullness:** belly roundness (fullness file).
- **Fat layer:** subcutaneous thickness (conditioning).
- **Separation:** depth and width of the grooves between muscles.
- **Micro-detail:** fine relief along the fiber direction ("grain").
- **Width vs. depth:** front-view breadth vs. side-view depth per region.

## 1. Muscle maturity ("mature muscle", "mature physique")

### Meaning
A finished, detailed, hard look that people link to many years of training: "the 'look' of your muscles after a certain number of years of training" [17]. Judges also use it for holding a full look through posing. Weinberger said Lunsford "does not have the muscle maturity of Hadi, he couldn't withstand all the posing and started to flatten out" [22].

### Visible features
- Fine "grainy detail" and "striations, splits and grooves" on delts, chest, quads, glutes, and back [20].
- A surface that looks "hard and solid", not "smooth or puffy" [19].
- A "more seasoned, dense look even if the tape measure or the scale may not indicate any changes" [20], that is, at the same size.
- Often rounder bellies and more back depth [25].

### Confused neighbors
- Density, hardness, graininess: treated as parts of maturity [19, 20], or listed as separate items [24].
- Conditioning: Cutler separates "the density versus the conditioning factor" [21].
- Fullness (Weinberger's sense) [22]. Size, which comes "fairly quickly" (forum) [37].

### Consistency of usage
Mostly consistent as a look, but with two senses and a disputed cause. No rulebook uses it [1–12]. Writers admit "there is no scientific definition for muscle maturity" [19] and that research "is sparse to nonexistent" [17].

### Causes: supported vs. speculative
- *Supported:* Training makes muscle bigger, with larger pennation angles [38, 39]. Fiber number stays normal, and connective tissue stays about 13% of the muscle [41]. Growth slows in trained people [67, 44]. Visible detail needs a thin fat layer [53, 55].
- *Plausible but unproven:* skin change. Skin collagen falls with age, and dermal thickness tracks it [56]. No study measured skin in bodybuilders. The one training trial on skin found no significant dermal-thickness change [110].
- *Folklore:* "packed denser" muscle. Elite bodybuilders had lower myofibrillar volume density [42] and lower fiber specific tension [45]. Training lowered actin and myosin concentration [46]. The topic is open [47]. Fibers do not "morph" from IIa to IIb [48].
- *No direct evidence found:* a definition or test of maturity; a visible fascia or connective-tissue change in humans [49, 50].

### Honest illustrative representation
Make "maturity" a preset of other controls: more micro-detail, more secondary splits, slightly rounder bellies, more back depth. Lock size, frame, and fat layer. Caption: "Same size and fat. Only surface detail changed. The cause is not known." Regions: delts, pec–delt tie-in, triceps, quads, glutes, lower back. Avoid an age slider, "denser tissue" animations, and weight changes.

### Classification
Overlapping slang; contested explanation.

## 2. Density ("dense")

### Meaning
Muscle that looks solid and packed, not soft or "watery". People also use it for back depth ("back density") and for the amount of muscle on a frame.

### Visible features
- Tight, compact muscle with deep borders and some fine detail.
- Back poses: mid- and lower-back detail and depth. Manion: "He added so much more density to his back." [29]
- Side poses: chest and back depth [14, 30].
- The opposite, per Yates: muscles like "balloons filled with water, not solid and dense at all" [15].

### Confused neighbors
- Thickness: "I settled for width over density" [14]; "thickness and density" [30].
- Amount of muscle: "a densely muscled physique" under Size [8].
- Leanness: "lean muscle tissue ... in comparison to the amount of fatty tissue" [36].
- Maturity, hardness, conditioning [21, 24].
- Science terms: CT "muscle density" marks fat inside muscle [57, 58]. Literal tissue density is about 1.06 g/cm³ [61].

### Consistency of usage
Official but undefined. IFBB lists density under muscle "quality" [1] and asks Men's Physique for "overall body density" [3]. Sources use at least four meanings. Overlapping slang.

### Causes: supported vs. speculative
- *Supported:* the look is not literal density. Muscle is about 1.06 g/cm³ (fixed samples 1.055–1.112) [61]; fat is 0.900 g/cm³ [62].
- *Supported, other meaning:* CT density falls as muscle fat rises (lean 49.2 vs. obese 39.3 HU) [57] and shifts with training status [59]. It is not visible through skin. Echo intensity rose in one dieting pro without fat gain [60].
- *Supported contributors:* more muscle and low fat. Bodybuilders had larger shoulder, chest, and arm girths than weightlifters with similar lean mass and fat [72]. Contest fat is low [54, 55].
- *Folklore:* tighter packing of contractile protein [42, 45, 46].
- *No direct evidence found:* any measure that matches visual "density".

### Honest illustrative representation
Build "dense" as a composite at fixed size and fat: deeper borders, slightly rounder bellies, mild micro-detail. For "back density", add side-view depth to the erector and mid-back region, and keep lat width fixed. Do not change mass, weight, or a material "density" value. Best views: rear double biceps, side chest, quads.

### Classification
Overlapping slang; visual description; contested explanation (when tied to tissue packing).

## 3. Hardness ("hard" vs. "soft")

### Meaning
"Hard" muscle looks firm, tight, and sharply detailed, mostly when flexed. "Soft" muscle looks less hard and less detailed. People blame fat, water, or low glycogen for softness, so the word names no single cause (as in the fullness file).

### Visible features
- Hard: crisp edges, visible splits and striations, shape held in poses.
- Soft: rounded transitions, shallow and blurred grooves, a smooth outline.
- Clearest on quads, glutes, hamstrings, abs, and delts.

### Confused neighbors
- Dry, crispy, conditioned (other files); dense, grainy, mature.
- Smooth: IFBB wants Men's Physique "'softer' and 'smoother'" than bodybuilding [3].
- Scientific "muscle hardness" (durometer, elastography) measures skin, fat, and muscle together [63] and rises after damaging exercise [64]. It is not the stage look.

### Consistency of usage
Mostly consistent as a visual description. IFBB Men's Physique rejects the "dryness or hardness that are seen at bodybuilding competitions" [3]. OCB Classic "should not be as lean, striated or hard" [9]. WNBF wants observations ("need more separation and hardness in the legs"), not causes ("be more conditioned or dryer") [7]. Weinberger: "let's say 10 percent softer" [28]. The causes are disputed.

### Causes: supported vs. speculative
- *Supported:* a thin fat layer exposes edges. Lean athletes can have only a few millimeters of fat summed over eight sites [53]. Male contest body fat is about 5.8–10.7% [55].
- *Plausible but unproven:* water and glycogen effects (fullness file).
- *Coach claim, no direct evidence:* heavy low reps raise "myogenic tone" for a harder look [31]. Resting stiffness did not change after 6 weeks [65] and rose after 8 weeks [66]. No study links stiffness to the "hard" look.
- *Risk note:* dehydration or electrolyte tricks to look harder are unstudied for appearance and "may be a dangerous practice" [71]. This file gives no methods.

### Honest illustrative representation
Map hard ↔ soft to edge sharpness and groove contrast, and blur micro-detail at the soft end. Keep size fixed. Keep the fat-layer control separate, so users see that "soft" has more than one claimed cause. Do not add touch stiffness. Best regions: quads, glutes, hamstrings, delts, abs.

### Classification
Visual description; overlapping slang (with dense, dry, conditioned); contested explanation (cause).

## 4. Graininess ("grainy")

### Meaning
Very fine, close lines across the muscles, like grain in wood. It is a sign of extreme conditioning. Yates: "Prior to me becoming Mr. Olympia, the term 'grainy' had never been used to describe a bodybuilder's condition." [15]

### Visible features
- Thin parallel lines along each muscle's fiber direction: fanning down the delts, toward the armpit on the pecs, and along the glutes, hamstrings, quads, triceps, and upper back.
- Seen over much of the body at once: "An overall grainy look throughout the physique should be seen." [8]
- Visible only with a very thin fat layer.

### Confused neighbors
- Striations: the single lines (see `detail-vascularity.md`). "Grainy" means many fine striations almost everywhere.
- Dry, crispy (fullness file).
- Dense, mature: "very thin skin stretched over super dense muscles" [15]; "muscle so dense and skin so thin that observers coined the term 'grainy'" [16].
- Meat-science "grain" = fascicle size set by the perimysium [52].

### Consistency of usage
Mostly consistent for the look. OCB lists it under Definition [8]. NPC Worldwide lists "grainy" among "common terms used in the bodybuilding industry" [6]. The causes are disputed.

### Causes: supported vs. speculative
- *Supported:* it needs a very thin fat layer. Elite male bodybuilders had 36.7 mm summed over eight skinfolds before a contest [54]. Skinfolds include skin [53].
- *Plausible but unproven:* the lines follow fascicles or perimysium walls, which connect to the muscle's outer sheath at the surface [51]. No study maps stage-visible lines to this anatomy.
- `[INFERENCE]` The lines are not single fibers. Elite bodybuilder fibers average about 9,600 µm² [43], or about 0.11 mm across.
- *Folklore:* "very thin skin" and "years of extremely heavy and intense training" as causes [15]. Skin thickness in bodybuilders is unmeasured.

### Honest illustrative representation
Use a high-frequency normal map aligned to each muscle's fiber direction. Fade it in only when the fat layer is thin. Keep it finer than the separation grooves. Caption: "Lines follow muscle-bundle direction. Which tissue makes them visible is not known." Do not draw single fibers or a "skin thickness" cause slider. Regions: delts, upper pecs, glutes, hamstrings, quads, triceps, upper back.

### Classification
Visual description; overlapping slang (with maturity, dense, striated); contested explanation.

## 5. Size vs. shape ("muscle shape", "genetic shape", "full bellies")

### Meaning
Size is how much muscle there is. Shape is the outline and curve of each muscle and of the whole body: "your shoulders into your waist, into your thighs, ... the shape of your muscles" [26]; "the aesthetic quality and curvature of individual muscle groups" [12].

### Visible features
- Muscle level: biceps peak, "capped" delts, outer-quad sweep, calf shape, full vs. flat bellies. IFBB looks for "a full, peaked development" of the biceps [1] and, in Classic, "a round and firm appearance" [2].
- Body level: the shoulder–waist–thigh outline and the tie-ins.

### Confused neighbors
Insertions (section 6), proportion (section 7), fullness (fullness file), and mass: "Nick is a mass monster. Shape is not his forte." [27]

### Consistency of usage
Mostly consistent. Judges separate "shape, size, and condition" [26]. They split shape into "genetic shape which your mother and father gave you" and "the muscle you put on that helps improve your shape" [26].

### Causes: supported vs. speculative
- *Supported:* size is heritable (muscle mass h² = 0.81) [81]. Gains from the same program range from −2% to +59% [68].
- *Supported:* growth is uneven along and between muscles [73–75, 78, 79], but long-length training changes regional growth only a little on average [76]. "Many exercises per muscle" is speculation [77].
- *Supported (small studies):* muscle shape is similar between people. Vastii shape did not depend on size or sex [84]. Arm volume distribution was conserved across a threefold size range [83].
- `[INFERENCE]` Physique shape differences come more from size, bone and belly lengths, and fat than from muscle shape.
- *No direct evidence found:* heritability of muscle shape; a visible shape change from regional growth. The closest data are one small twin study of calf fascicles [82].

### Honest illustrative representation
Separate a uniform "size" control per muscle from small "shape" presets (biceps peak vs. long flat belly; outer sweep vs. teardrop). Keep shape changes small next to size changes. Never tie shape presets to moving attachments. Regions: biceps, delts, quads, calves.

### Classification
Distinct concepts (size vs. shape); shape is also a visual description with subjective parts.

## 6. Insertions ("high/low insertions", "high biceps", "low lats", "gaps")

### Meaning
In bodybuilding, "insertion" means where the fleshy belly ends and the tendon starts. A "low" insertion is a long belly that reaches near the joint. A "high" insertion is a short belly with a long tendon and a visible gap. In anatomy, the insertion is the tendon's footprint on bone [85].

### Visible features
- Biceps: a gap above the elbow crease at 90° flexion. "If you can squeeze four fingers in there, for example, that is bad in terms of muscle length" [32]. "A larger gap usually suggests shorter biceps." [33]
- Calves: a short belly sits high over a long Achilles tendon.
- Lats: "low lats" seem to reach toward the waist in back poses.
- Long bellies make muscles "flow into each other" [32].

### Confused neighbors
Shape and peak (a peak is height; an insertion is length). Anatomical vs. bodybuilding "insertion". Old advice to "Emphasize the origins and insertions of the muscles when you train" [35].

### Consistency of usage
Mostly consistent in bodybuilding: insertions are structural and fixed. "the muscle insertion and origin points, they are fixed" [32]; insertions "are just about entirely genetic" [33]. The word clashes with anatomy. We verified no named-source definition of "low lats".

### Causes: supported vs. speculative
- *Supported:* muscle–tendon anatomy varies: two-part distal biceps tendons in 10 of 17 elbows [86], a third biceps head in 10% of arms [87], and longer Achilles tendons in Kenyans from age 8 [88].
- *Supported:* belly length follows the skeleton [92, 83].
- *Supported:* training changes tendon stiffness more than size [89, 90]. "no such reports exist for an exercise-induced change of tendon rest length" [90]. Exercise did not change attachment sites in sheep [91].
- *Plausible but unproven:* belly length is genetic [88]. Short bellies cap volume, because volume ≈ shape factor × largest cross-section × length [84].
- *No direct evidence found:* heritability of belly or tendon length; studies of "high biceps", "low lats", or "gaps".

### Honest illustrative representation
Add a "belly–tendon split" control per muscle that moves the muscle–tendon junction along a fixed tendon path. Bones and attachments stay fixed. At equal volume, a shorter belly bulges more and leaves a bigger gap. Show biceps at 90°, calves from behind, and lats in a rear lat spread. Label it "structure that varies between people", not a training result.

### Classification
Distinct concept (structural); the slang meaning differs from the anatomical meaning.

## 7. Symmetry, balance, and proportion

### Meaning
- Strict symmetry: the left side matches the right. "Symmetry – the relative proportion of the left and right sides of the physique." [12]
- Bodybuilding "symmetry" usually means overall shape and balance: "symmetry as it applied to bodybuilding seemed to describe more" [14].
- Balance: the upper vs. lower halves. Proportion: "the relative size of one individual part of the physique as compared to another" [12].

### Visible features
Matching left and right arms, pecs, quads, and calves. Legs that match the upper body. A front that matches the back. Even part ratios: delts to waist, arms to legs, calves to thighs. IFBB asks for "limbs and trunk in good proportion" [1].

### Confused neighbors
Shape, aesthetics, X-frame. WNBF symmetry covers "left and right, front to back, and upper and lower body", "emphasizing V-taper and X-frame" [7]. ExRx calls the broad usage "A misnomer" [13].

### Consistency of usage
Disputed. WFF uses the strict sense [12]. WNBF uses every direction [7]. NANBF means "structural harmony and overall balance" [10]. BNBF means "the overall shape, structure and proportion" [11]. NPC scores "a balance of size, symmetry, and muscularity" without definitions [4]. Weinberger pairs "symmetry and the proportions" [27].

### Causes: supported vs. speculative
- *Supported:* one-sided sport makes left–right differences: dominant-arm muscles 11–15% larger in tennis players [107]; single-muscle asymmetries above 15% in sprinters [80].
- *Supported:* bone breadths are heritable [97, 98].
- *Plausible but unproven (weak):* body symmetry drives attractiveness. One 3D-scan study found strong links [105]. A meta-analysis found almost no link between asymmetry and masculinity [106]. The first author of [105] has a retracted symmetry paper [109].
- *No direct evidence found:* an ideal ratio used in judging.

### Honest illustrative representation
Strict symmetry: an "asymmetry" slider that enlarges one side's arm, pec, or quad by up to about 15% [107, 80]. Balance: shift volume between upper and lower body at a constant total. Proportion: per-region sliders with live part ratios. Show the federation definitions side by side, because they disagree.

### Classification
Distinct concept (strict left–right symmetry); visual description (a subjective judgement) for balance and proportion; overlapping slang ("symmetry" has two senses).

## 8. Aesthetics and "flow"

### Meaning
Aesthetics is how pleasing the whole body looks, usually against a "classical" ideal. IFBB favours "a harmonious, classical physique" [1]. IFBB Classic Bodybuilding seeks "a less muscular, yet athletic and aesthetically pleasing physique" [2]. "Flow" means muscles that blend into each other without gaps or blocky steps [32].

### Visible features
- A smooth outline from shoulder to waist to thigh.
- Smooth tie-ins: pec–delt, delt–arm, lat–waist, quad–knee.
- Full bellies that fill the space between joints.
- Few blocky breaks. OCB accepts "Some blockiness in the physique ... as long as it is not distracting to the overall flow of the physique" [8].

### Confused neighbors
Symmetry and proportion, shape, insertions (long bellies help flow [32]), V-taper and X-frame. General attractiveness research uses a different standard.

### Consistency of usage
Overlapping and subjective. Rulebooks use "aesthetically pleasing" and "harmonious" without definitions [1, 2]. "Flow" appears in NPC Worldwide ("the beauty and flow of the physique") [6], OCB [8], and BNBF ("a very strong shapely flowing physique") [11]. Zane wrote of "fluid lines" [14]. A coach says: "There's always a genetic component to aesthetics." [34]

### Causes: supported vs. speculative
- *Supported (general population, not bodybuilders):* a V-shaped torso predicts rated attractiveness. Waist-to-chest ratio explained 56% of the variance in Maisey et al., as summarized in [99]. Urban raters used it more than rural raters [100]. Speed-dating data agree [101]. Perceived strength explained over 70% of men's bodily attractiveness [102].
- *Conflicting:* one study rated moderate muscularity highest [103]. Men overestimate how much muscle women prefer [104].
- `[INFERENCE]` Bodybuilding aesthetics is a sport standard. It overlaps with everyday attractiveness, but it is not the same.
- *No direct evidence found:* "flow" as a measurable property.

### Honest illustrative representation
Add a "flow" overlay that draws the silhouette and marks sharp concave steps at the tie-ins. Longer bellies and balanced sizes reduce the steps. Label presets such as "classic" or "mass" as division styles, not as correct bodies.

### Classification
Visual description (a subjective aesthetic judgement); overlapping slang.

## 9. V-taper and X-frame

### Meaning
- V-taper: wide shoulders and back over a narrow waist. "The V-taper refers to your shoulder-to-waist ratio, particularly when viewed from the back." [34] The IFBB front lat spread creates "a V-shaped torso" [1].
- X-frame: a V-taper plus wide thighs: "wide shoulders, wide lats, small waist and hips, flaring 'sweep' on the thighs and diamond-shaped calves" [35]. WNBF: "Capped delts, wide lats, sweeping quads, making the appearance of a small waist" [7].

### Visible features
A front and back silhouette with broad delt caps and lats, a narrow waist and hips, outer-quad sweep, and shaped calves. WNBF judges consider "the silhouette of the athlete" first [7].

### Confused neighbors
Symmetry (WNBF puts both inside it [7]), proportion, aesthetics, width (section 10). A thick waist works against both: "A blocky, thick waist is not desirable." [9]

### Consistency of usage
Consistent as silhouette words. "V-shaped torso" is in the IFBB rules [1]. "X-frame" is in natural-federation rules [7] and magazines [34, 35], not in the IFBB or NPC texts we found [1, 4]. Writers treat the frame as fixed: "you can't change the proportions of your skeleton" [34].

### Causes: supported vs. speculative
- *Supported:* shoulder and hip bone breadths are heritable [97, 98]. Boys reach about 80% of clavicle length by age 12 [94]. Complete fusion of the inner clavicle growth plate was first seen at 21 in men [95]. Pelvic width may slowly grow with age [96].
- *Supported:* biacromial breadth is measured between the bony acromia [93], so visible shoulder width also includes the deltoids. Bodybuilders had larger shoulder girths than weightlifters with similar lean mass [72].
- *Supported (perception):* V-shaped torsos are rated attractive [99–101].
- *No direct evidence found:* training changes bone breadth; an ideal ratio for judging; "X-frame" in perception research.

### Honest illustrative representation
Keep the skeleton fixed. Build the V from soft tissue: delt cap width, lat flare, waist fat, and oblique size. Offer "frame" presets (narrow vs. broad clavicles or pelvis) as structure, apart from training sliders. Draw the live outline and the shoulder-to-waist ratio in front and back views. For the X, add outer-quad sweep and calf shape.

### Classification
Visual description (silhouette); distinct concept for the skeletal-frame part.

## 10. Size, "mass", and "thickness" (width vs. depth)

### Meaning
- Size and mass: how much muscle a body shows. ExRx's "Mass" entry: "The perception of muscular size, has been the foundation for bodybuilding" [13]. BNBF judges "muscular mass relative to frame size" [11]. WFF judges size "relative to the competitor's height" [12].
- Width is breadth in front or back view. Thickness is depth from the side and in the back. Weinberger: "If Chris had a thicker back, bigger arms, and a thicker chest, he could have won that show", though his "lat spread, if you just looked at a lat spread, it was better than Martin's" [28].
- "Thick" praises muscle ("he's thick and he's round. And that's bodybuilding." [28]) but criticizes a waist ("on the thick side") [27].

### Visible features
Width: front and back lat spreads. Thickness: side chest and side triceps ("The side of the leg gets judged in two poses" [29]) and back depth (erectors, traps, mid-back). Mass: overall bulk in every pose.

### Confused neighbors
Density (paired with thickness [30]; "width over density" [14]), fullness (roundness), and muscularity ("the amount of visible muscle" [12]).

### Consistency of usage
Width vs. thickness is consistent. Size and mass are used interchangeably. Rulebooks say "muscle size", "muscular bulk" [1], or "size" [4], never "thickness".

### Causes: supported vs. speculative
- *Supported (measurement):* research measures thickness (1D), cross-sectional area (2D), and volume (3D) separately [108]. Ultrasound thickness is the distance between the deep and superficial aponeuroses at mid-belly [108]. Thickness, width, and length "may hypertrophy differently" [108].
- *Supported:* bodybuilders have thicker triceps than untrained men [38]. Triceps thickness spans 6–66 mm across 711 people [40].
- *Plausible but unproven:* specific exercises build width vs. thickness (vertical pulls vs. rows and rack deadlifts) [30]. No direct evidence found.

### Honest illustrative representation
Give separate controls for front-view width and side-view depth per region (back, chest, arms, thighs), plus overall volume ("mass"). Show front and side views together. Keep the frame fixed. Best views: lat spreads for width; side chest, side triceps, and rear poses for depth.

### Classification
Distinct concepts (geometry); "size" and "mass" are overlapping slang.

### Academic
38. **Muscle-fiber pennation angles are greater in hypertrophied than in normal muscles** — Kawakami Y, Abe T, Fukunaga T, 1993. <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8365975/> — academic. Bodybuilders have thicker triceps and larger pennation angles.
39. **Comparison of the muscle fascicle length between resistance-trained and untrained individuals: cross-sectional observation** — Fukutani A, Kurihara T, 2015. <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26185743/> — academic. Trained people (incl. bodybuilders): thicker muscle, larger pennation, same fascicle length.
40. **Human skeletal muscle size and architecture: variability and interdependence** — Kawakami Y, Abe T, Kanehisa H, Fukunaga T, 2006. <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17039483/> — academic. Triceps thickness 6–66 mm across 711 people.
41. **Muscle fiber number in biceps brachii in bodybuilders and control subjects** — MacDougall JD, Sale DG, Alway SE, Sutton JR, 1984. <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/6520032/> — academic. Same fiber number; connective and noncontractile tissue about 13% in all groups.
42. **Muscle ultrastructural characteristics of elite powerlifters and bodybuilders** — MacDougall JD, Sale DG, Elder GC, Sutton JR, 1982. <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7199447/> — academic. Lower myofibrillar volume density in the elite group (possible steroid use noted).
43. **Contrasts in muscle and myofibers of elite male and female bodybuilders** — Alway SE, Grumbt WH, Gonyea WJ, Stray-Gundersen J, 1989. <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2759948/> — academic. Mean fiber area 9,607 µm² in elite men.
44. **Effects of resistance training on elbow flexors of highly competitive bodybuilders** — Alway SE, Grumbt WH, Stray-Gundersen J, Gonyea WJ, 1992. <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1592744/> — academic. No significant muscle CSA change in 24 weeks.
45. **Single muscle fibre contractile properties differ between body-builders, power athletes and control subjects** — Meijer JP, Jaspers RT, Rittweger J, Seynnes OR, et al., 2015. <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26388513/> — academic. Larger fibers with lower specific tension.
46. **Muscle fiber hypertrophy in response to 6 weeks of high-volume resistance training in trained young men is largely attributed to sarcoplasmic hypertrophy** — Haun CT, Vann CG, Osburn SC, et al., 2019. <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31166954/> — academic. Actin and myosin concentration fell while fibers grew.
47. **Sarcoplasmic Hypertrophy in Skeletal Muscle: A Scientific "Unicorn" or Resistance Training Adaptation?** — Roberts MD, Haun CT, Vann CG, Osburn SC, Young KC, 2020. <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32760293/> — academic. Myofibril packing vs. sarcoplasm expansion is an open question.

49. **Role of extracellular matrix in adaptation of tendon and skeletal muscle to mechanical loading** — Kjaer M, 2004. <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15044685/> — academic. Loading raises collagen turnover (mostly tendon data).
50. **Skeletal Muscle Extracellular Matrix – What Do We Know About Its Composition, Regulation, and Physiological Roles? 51. **The Structure and Role of Intramuscular Connective Tissue in Muscle Function** — Purslow PP, 2020. <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7248366/> — academic. Perimysium divides muscle into fascicles and joins the epimysium at the surface; fascicle size varies.

## Open questions / uncertainty

- No academic source defines or tests "maturity", bodybuilding "density", "hardness", or "grain". PubMed searches for each term with "bodybuilding" returned nothing relevant. Judges assess all four by eye.
- The anatomy behind visible lines is not mapped. Fascicles and perimysium walls are a plausible guess [51], not a finding.
- Skin thickness in bodybuilders has not been measured. "Thin skin" stays unproven. The only resistance-training skin trial we found used facial skin in sedentary women, and its dermal-thickness change was not significant in its own table [110].
- Training-years data are small, self-reported, and not significant [69, 70]. No data link age to looks.
- Ultrastructure data in bodybuilders are small and old, and possible drug use confounds them [42, 45].
- No heritability data exist for muscle shape or for belly and tendon length. No human study tested whether training moves attachment sites.
- Regional hypertrophy is measured by MRI or ultrasound, not by appearance. No study tested whether regional growth changes visible shape.
- Symmetry–attractiveness evidence is weak, and one key author has a retraction [105, 109].
- Many judge and coach quotes come from transcriptions of videos or podcasts [21, 22, 26–29]. We did not review the videos. The IFBB Pro League publishes its criteria as videos, which we did not review [5].
- Usage of "low lats" and "high/low insertions" was found mostly on blogs and forums; we verified no named-source definition for "low lats".
