# Fullness and water terms: full, flat, spilled over, pumped, dry vs. watery, peak week

Compiled 2026-10-09. Every URL in the Sources and Visual references lists was fetched on that date, and the cited claim was found on the page.

Scope: this file covers full, flat, spilled over, pumped vs. unpumped, dry vs. watery/soft/smooth/"holding water", and peak week. Neighbor terms are in other files: `conditioning.md` (lean, shredded, "dry and crispy"), `maturity-shape.md` (mature, dense, hard, grainy), and `detail-vascularity.md` (separation, striations, veins).

How to read this file:
- Each term separates (a) the visual impression, (b) the cause that the community claims, and (c) what the evidence supports.
- [n] points to the Sources list. **[INFERENCE]** marks our own reasoning. No source states it.
- "No direct evidence found" means that we searched and found no study.
- This file gives no water, salt, carbohydrate, diuretic, or drug protocols, amounts, or schedules.

## Key questions: short answers

1. **Is "flat" the same as "depleted"?** No. The two words overlap. "Depleted" names a diet state: low muscle glycogen after a long deficit or a depletion phase [61][53]. "Flat" names a look: smaller, less round muscles. Sources blame a flat look on low glycogen, dehydration, long posing, cold, over-pumping, or a dull skin surface [56][61][66][73][67][76]. Low glycogen makes muscles a little smaller, by about 2–4% in thickness [12][13][14]. Dehydration shrinks worked muscles [20]. No study has tested what observers call "flat".
2. **Can the eye tell "spillover" from "soft" or "smooth"?** We found no evidence that it can. Coaches agree on the look (blurred lines, skin less tight), but they disagree on the cause [56][57][58][59]. Several coaches say that the label often covers excess body fat [60][75][3]. Reviews call the mechanism an athletes' theory or an anecdotal opinion [2][3]. No study has measured it.
3. **Does carbohydrate loading change muscle size?** Yes, a little. MRI and ultrasound show about 2–5% more muscle thickness or cross-sectional area [10][12][13][14]. A tape measure often shows no change [11][14]. The only study with blinded judges found better silhouette scores only in the loaders (4.5 to 5.1), but the group-by-time interaction was not significant [10].
4. **Are changes of water under the skin visible?** No direct evidence found. In clinical work, edema becomes detectable only after interstitial fluid rises by about 30% (4–5 kg of body weight) [24]. Poor skin turgor has no proven value to detect low body water in adults [25]. A ±2% change in body mass from water did not change ultrasound fat thickness [22]. In one single-athlete case, the authors report 10% less summed subcutaneous thickness from 3 days out to show day, but the show-day sum (29.6) was about the same as the sum 6 days out (29.8) [14].
5. **Lighting, tan, oil, and pose.** Rulebooks control them [48][50][51][52]. Coaches say that they change how full or dry a body looks [67][69]. Perception studies show that light direction and gloss change perceived 3D shape [39]–[44]. No study has tested these effects on muscular bodies. See "Presentation effects" below.

## 1. Full / fullness

1. **Meaning** – The muscles look as large and round as the athlete can show, with tight skin over them. Most coaches tie it to stored muscle glycogen and the water stored with it [56][63][1].
2. **Visible features** – Round, convex bellies on the pecs, delts, arms, quad sweep, and glutes. The muscles look like they are "pushing out tightly against the skin" [56]. Flexed poses show a clear peak: IFBB judges look for "a full, peaked development" in the front double biceps [48]. The shape holds through long posing; a judge called the opposite "started to flatten out" [66].
3. **Confused neighbors** – *Pumped*: lasts minutes; "full" lasts through the day. *Big* or *mass*: how much muscle exists, not how filled it looks. *Spilled over*: full "but not necessarily as tight" [62]. *Mature* or *dense*: the ability to stay full during posing (`maturity-shape.md`).
4. **Consistency of usage** – Consistent for the look. Mostly consistent for the cause. Rulebooks use "full" as praise [48][54][55]. A review defines it as "the muscle cell fuel stores are fully repleted / super compensated" [1].
5. **Causes: supported vs. speculative**
   - Supported, small effect: glycogen loading raised thigh cross-sectional area by 3.5% on MRI (n = 5) [12]. Bodybuilders who loaded gained about 3% upper-arm thickness; non-loaders did not (groups not randomized) [10]. A 4-person crossover trial found +2.9%, possibly within measurement error [13]. Each gram of glycogen holds about 2.7–4 g of water, and loading raised the water inside cells, not outside them [16][17].
   - Unproven: that judges see this change. Girth often does not change [11]. In one case, arm girth stayed at 40.5 cm while self-rated fullness rose from 1 to 8 out of 10 [14]. Reviews say that no data link the measured changes to visual changes [2][1].
   - Folklore: sodium or water tricks add fullness. They are not shown to work or to be safe [1][4].
6. **Honest illustrative representation** – Push the muscle bellies outward along their own surface, most at mid-belly. Keep bones, joints, tendons, insertions, skin lines, and fat layer fixed, so that "full" reads as volume, not leanness. Offer a "measured" setting (about 2–4% thickness) and a labeled "exaggerated for teaching" setting. Best views: side chest (pecs, delts), front double biceps (biceps peak), front pose (quads).
7. **Classification** – Visual description; contested explanation (the size effect is supported; the visual link is unproven).

## 2. Flat / flatness

1. **Meaning** – The muscles look smaller, less round, and "deflated" compared with the athlete's best look. People use the word for the late diet, for the hours after a pump fades, and for a bad stage day.
2. **Visible features** – Less convex bellies, straighter outlines, and less "pop" when flexed, mostly in the pecs, delts, arms, quad sweep, and glutes. Fewer visible veins: flat is "the opposite of the 'pump'" [64]. Very lean athletes can look "stringy": the lines show, but the bellies look thin [55][57]. Sharp detail can remain. Flat is not the same as smooth.
3. **Confused neighbors** – *Depleted*: a diet state, not a look. A dieter "is arguably already depleted" [61], and a loaded but dehydrated athlete can still look flat [61][1]. *Over-dieted*: real muscle loss; a judge "lost muscle size ... very lean and not full" [74]. *Soft* or *smooth*: lost detail, not lost volume, but both can occur together: "flat ... a bit watery" [65]. *Unpumped*: the normal rested state.
4. **Consistency of usage** – The look is consistent. The cause is disputed. Sources name low glycogen [56][63][53], dehydration [61][1][6], long posing [66], cold [73], over-pumping [67][76], and no oil shine [67]. Rulebooks treat "flat" as a fault [52][55]. Lay lifters use "depleted", "flat", "full", and "bloated" as one loose set [77].
5. **Causes: supported vs. speculative**
   - Supported, small effect: low glycogen lowers muscle thickness by a few percent [13][14]. Dehydration from exercise in heat shrank knee-extensor volume by 3.9–5.9% on MRI; arms and delts did not change [20].
   - Unproven: that these changes are what people call "flat". No study has rated "flat". A bodybuilder in an interview study said, "If you were dehydrated, the muscles are going to look smaller as well" [6].
   - No direct evidence found: cold, posing fatigue, or over-pumping as causes.
   - **[INFERENCE]** Low glycogen and true muscle loss look alike from the outside.
6. **Honest illustrative representation** – Reduce belly convexity by a few percent and lower the flexed peak. Keep skin lines, separations, and the fat layer unchanged, to show that flat is not soft. Reduce vein relief a little. Never change the skeleton. Label the state "several possible causes". Best regions: pecs, delts, arms, quad sweep, and glutes.
7. **Classification** – Visual description; contested explanation; overlaps with "depleted".

## 3. Spilled over / spillover

1. **Meaning** – A peak that "went too far": the muscles look full, but the lines blur and the skin looks less tight. The community blames too much carbohydrate, sodium, or water, which supposedly pushes water under the skin [56][57][58][59].
2. **Visible features (as coaches describe them)** – Blurred muscle borders, fewer striations, and less separation: "excess subcutaneous water ... blurs definition and reduces striations and separation between muscles" [56]. Also "soft and bloated" [57], "watery or bloated" [59], and full "but not necessarily as tight" [62]. One coach lists anecdotal "hot spots": blurry ab segments, a larger waist, no oblique striations, and "balloon looking" shoulders and chest [57]. No other source names a consistent first site.
3. **Confused neighbors** – *Soft or smooth from fat*: "if you think it's water, it's not, that's just body fat!" [60]; "It's tough to spill if you are truly in shape" [56]; "no, you were just fat" [75]. Review authors give the opinion that smoothness is "typically the result of excess body fat or loading too many carbohydrates" [3]. *Flat*: the label gets used "when there is no understanding as to what went wrong" [75]. *Watery*: the wider complaint; "spilled" implies an overshoot after loading. *Training inflammation*: it "could blur muscle definition on stage" [9].
4. **Consistency of usage** – Disputed. Sources agree on the look, but not on the cause: carbohydrate [57][59], sodium [58], or "water control" [75]. The label often covers a physique that was not lean enough [60][75].
5. **Causes: supported vs. speculative**
   - No direct evidence found that excess carbohydrate moves water under the skin, or that such water is visible. Reviews call it what "physique athletes theorise" [2] or anecdotal opinion [3]. A survey cites coaching books for it [5]. A coach writes, "I cannot find a scientific reference to support this" [57].
   - Measured data do not settle it. In one study, loading raised the water inside cells, but not outside them [16]. In the single-athlete case, estimates of water outside cells rose during loading. The method cannot show where that water is, and the error "may equate, or even be greater than" the changes [14].
   - Plausible pieces only: high-carbohydrate feeding causes short-lived sodium retention [27], but high salt intake moved fluid out of the interstitial space into the blood [26]. Neither study measured skin or appearance.
   - Visibility limits: clinical edema needs about 30% more interstitial fluid before it is detectable [24]. A ±2% water change did not change ultrasound fat thickness [22].
6. **Honest illustrative representation** – Keep the "full" volume. Reduce groove depth and edge sharpness at borders, fade striations, and smooth the surface, mostly at the abs, obliques, lower back, glute–hamstring tie-in, and pec–delt tie-in. Never draw a water layer or moving fluid. Use the same surface controls as "soft from fat", and say so on screen: "The eye cannot tell these apart; the water explanation is not demonstrated."
7. **Classification** – Overlapping slang; contested explanation.

## 4. Pumped vs. unpumped

1. **Meaning** – "Pumped" is the short-term swelling and tightness of a muscle after repeated contractions. Competitors "pump up" backstage just before they go on stage. "Unpumped" is not a rulebook term; it is the rested state.
2. **Visible features** – Bigger, rounder, tight-looking muscles in the worked areas, which backstage are mostly the arms, delts, chest, and upper back [67][68][76]. "You look bigger, harder and more vascular" [62]. Veins stand out more (common vein sites are in `detail-vascularity.md`). A contest-day guide says to avoid pumping the quads and abs and to "just flex" them [68].
3. **Confused neighbors** – *Full*: a state that lasts; a pump fades within minutes. *Vascular* or *dry*: a pump raises the veins, which people can read as "dry". *Flat*: "the opposite of the 'pump'" [64]. *Delayed swelling*: swelling from muscle damage comes hours to days later and lasts days. It is not a pump [36][37].
4. **Consistency of usage** – The look and the mechanism are consistent. The timing is disputed: 5–10 min [67], 10 or 30 min [59], or 30–60 min [5] before stage. Most coaches warn that over-pumping flattens or smooths the legs and arms [67][68][76].
5. **Causes: supported vs. speculative**
   - Supported: exercise moves fluid from the blood into the working muscle. Right after squats, vasti cross-sectional area rose 10% and adductors 5%, while plasma volume fell 22% [28]. At maximal effort, the largest water gain is inside the muscle cells [29]. A review calls this cell swelling "the pump" [35]. Ultrasound thickness rose about 7–19% [30][31][33], also in resistance-trained men [33].
   - Time course: thickness peaks at 0–5 min and falls toward baseline within 15–30 min [30][31]. Quadriceps volume was still about 6.5% up 30 min after sprints [32]. In one study, thickness stayed up for 60 min after heavy leg work [34].
   - Veins: heat and prolonged exercise widen the superficial arm veins [38]. No study measured vein visibility after resistance exercise.
   - No direct evidence found: that a pump-up improves judged appearance, or that over-pumping flattens a physique.
6. **Honest illustrative representation** – Make it a time-based effect. Enlarge only the worked bellies (arms, delts, chest, lats) by a modest amount and raise vein relief. Fade both back over about 30 minutes. Keep the fat layer and separations unchanged. A slight skin flush is optional **[INFERENCE]**; no source measured it.
7. **Classification** – Distinct concept (measured and well described). "Unpumped" is a plain description, not slang.

## 5. Dry vs. watery / soft / smooth / "holding water"

1. **Meaning** – "Dry" is a hard, detailed look: thin-looking skin, deep lines, striations, and visible veins. "Watery", "holding water", "soft", and "smooth" name the opposite: blurred lines and a softer surface. People blame water under the skin [1], fat [60], poor sleep, or stress [58].
2. **Visible features** – *Dry*: deep grooves at muscle borders, fine fiber-direction lines (striations), visible veins (sites for both are in `detail-vascularity.md`), and "a dry, hard, grainy physique" [70][71]. *Watery / soft / smooth*: shallow, soft-edged grooves, few striations, fewer veins, and a puffy look; coaches name the waist, lower abs, obliques, and lower body [57][60].
3. **Confused neighbors** – *Lean / conditioned*: "The leaner you are, the dryer you get" [70] (`conditioning.md`). *Thin skin*: "Thin-skinned individuals (literally speaking) will generally have a more striated, defined appearance" [71]. *Mature, grainy, hard*: `maturity-shape.md`. *Spilled over*: one story about a watery look after loading.
4. **Consistency of usage** – *Dry*: the look is mostly consistent; the cause is disputed; the value differs by division. IFBB Men's Physique marks down "too dry" and wants a "softer" and "smoother" look [49]. A natural federation discourages extreme "dryness" [54]. WNBF tells judges not to say "be dryer" [52]. *Watery / holding water*: the look is mostly consistent; the cause is disputed. *Soft*, *smooth*: overlapping slang; "smooth" also describes skin, tan, and posing [49][52].
5. **Causes: supported vs. speculative**
   - No direct evidence found that judged dryness tracks water under the skin. The effect of dehydration and electrolyte manipulation on appearance "has not been studied" [4]. Water and sodium practices are not shown to be "specifically effective and/or safe" [1].
   - Indirect evidence: ±2% water changes did not change ultrasound fat thickness [22]. Mild dehydration thinned skin and hip soft tissue (about 7%) in non-athletes, but fat got thicker in some [23]. Dehydration in heat shrinks worked muscle [20]; small daily water changes do not [21]. Over 12 weeks of prep, biceps skin thickness fell on ultrasound while muscle thickness held [15], mostly from fat loss **[INFERENCE]**.
   - Show-day bioimpedance found more water inside cells and less outside them [19]. But the method has wide individual error [24] and cannot tell muscle water from under-skin water [2].
   - **[INFERENCE]** Body fat is the best-supported cause of a smooth look: fat lies between the skin and the muscle, and coaches often blame it [60][3].
   - Risks: water cutting and diuretics can cause dangerous electrolyte changes, paralysis, and heart-rhythm problems [46][47]. About 62% of physique athletes in one study were significantly or severely dehydrated on competition day (reported in [14]).
6. **Honest illustrative representation** – One "surface definition" control: groove depth and sharpness, striation strength, vein relief, and thinner-looking skin. Dry = sharper; watery or smooth = softer. Keep muscle volume fixed, so that dry is not full. Use the same control for "soft from fat", because the cause is invisible. Never draw a water layer. Best regions: abs, obliques, serratus, lower back, glute–hamstring tie-in, quads, delts, and pecs.
7. **Classification** – *Dry*: visual description; contested explanation. *Watery / holding water*: overlapping slang; contested explanation. *Soft / smooth*: overlapping slang.

## 6. Peak week (concept)

1. **Meaning** – The last days (often about a week) before a show. Competitors change food, carbohydrate, water, salt, and training to look their best on stage. The stated goals are full muscles, a "dry" look, and less abdominal bloating [1][3].
2. **Visible features (claimed targets)** – Larger, rounder muscle bellies (full). Sharper borders and thinner-looking skin (dry). A flat waist [1]. A good pump, tan, and oil at show time.
3. **Confused neighbors** – *Contest prep*: the months of fat loss that create most of the condition; "Peak week cannot make your physique, but it can break it" [59]. *Carb-up, water load, refeed*: practices inside peak week. *Pump-up*: minutes before stage.
4. **Consistency of usage** – Consistent target look. The disputes concern the methods and the size of the effect [59][61][2].
5. **Causes: supported vs. speculative**
   - Common practice: of 81 natural UK bodybuilders, 93.8% used a peaking strategy; 82.7% loaded carbohydrate, 65.4% loaded water, and 32.1% restricted water [5]. Of 160 athletes, 45% used a carbohydrate "back load" and 40.6% water loading; "PW strategies lack experimental evidence" [7]. Competitors in another study substantially reduced water in the final 3 days [8].
   - Measured: carbohydrate loading adds about 2–5% muscle thickness [10][13][14]. Body-water data come only from bioimpedance [19].
   - Not measured: an effect on placings. Four experienced natural bodybuilders said that a classic depletion and load "did not produce significant changes in appearance" [6].
   - Water and sodium: not shown to be effective or safe [1][4]. Removing sodium "will not have a significant impact on extracellular fluid removal" [3].
   - Risks: a case report describes paralysis from low potassium after peak-week carbohydrate loading with salt and fluid restriction [47]. Diuretic misuse causes electrolyte and heart-rhythm problems [46].
6. **Honest illustrative representation** – Not one slider. Show a timeline that drives the other controls (full, flat, dry/watery, pump). Give each change an evidence badge, for example "measured: small thickness change" or "claimed: water under skin, not measured". Keep lighting, tan, and oil fixed, or put them on a separate "presentation" track.
7. **Classification** – Distinct concept (a practice and a time window); contested explanation of its visual effects.

## Presentation effects: lighting, tan, oil, pose

- Rules: IFBB rules call for one even lighting system, no shadowed areas, and an average of 4,500–5,000 lux on stage [50]. The tan must look natural, and oil is allowed "in moderation" [48][51]. WNBF head judges move athletes to "accommodate for imperfect stage lighting" [52]. WNBF also notes that bodybuilders "who pose well" make "certain muscles' size more prominent" [52]. IFBB judges must remember that condition can change between prejudging and finals [48].
- Coaches: a dark tan under sharp light gives a "harder" look [69]. Too light a tan looks "washed out" and soft, and a pro with no oil shine "looked really flat" [67]. Hard posing under hot lights can itself pump the muscles [67]. Fans blamed poor backstage conditions (dim light, cold, crowding) for athletes who came in "off or watery" [72].
- Perception science (indirect; not tested on muscular bodies): people assume that light comes from above and slightly left [39], but visible lighting cues override that assumption [40]. A change of light direction changes perceived surface relief [41]. Gloss (specular highlights, like oil) carries shape information and can enhance perceived curvature [42][43], but gloss can also distort perceived shape [44]. A dark tan made a woman look thinner to male raters [45].
- No direct evidence found on how tan, oil, or lighting change perceived muscularity, fullness, or dryness.
- **[INFERENCE]** App rule: keep lighting, tan, oil, and pose fixed when a user toggles a physiology term. Put them on separate "presentation" controls, so users see that presentation alone can create the same impression.

## Open questions / uncertainty

- No study has rated "full", "flat", "spilled", "dry", or "watery" as visual outcomes. No study has tested whether judges agree on them.
- Is a 2–4% change in muscle thickness visible to judges under stage light? Unknown. Coaches judge glycogen status by eye ("flat" vs. "full") [63][1], but no validated tool reads glycogen from the outside; even ultrasound software failed [18].
- Can trained observers tell water under the skin from fat? No study found.
- Where does extra water go after a large carbohydrate intake in lean athletes? Bioimpedance cannot locate it, and no imaging study of the skin layer exists.
- How long does a real backstage pump-up last, and does it change placings? No study found.
- How do tan, oil, and light direction change perceived muscularity? Only indirect perception studies exist.
- Most peak-week data come from small samples (n = 1 to 24), self-selected groups, and often athletes who were not drug-tested.
- The classic "3–4 g of water per g of glycogen" figure goes back to Olsson & Saltin (1970). We could not read that paper and cite later studies [16][17].
- Searches with no relevant result included: "spill over" carbohydrate subcutaneous water study; study photographs rated muscle definition after dehydration or water loading; carbohydrate overfeeding subcutaneous swelling in lean men; bodybuilding judging reliability; tan or oil effect on perceived muscularity.
